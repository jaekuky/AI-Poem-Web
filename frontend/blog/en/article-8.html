The Day Computers Started Writing Poetry: A History of AI Poetry

Author: Jaekuk Ryu | AI & Poem Editorial Team
Published: April 2, 2026 | Last Updated: April 2, 2026

by Ryu

While building AI & Poem, I found myself drawn to a fundamental question: "When did the idea of computers writing poetry first emerge?" Most people think of AI poetry generators as a recent innovation from the past few years. But the ambition to have computers create art is nearly as old as computers themselves. As soon as the first commercial computers appeared, pioneering researchers were already experimenting with making machines write.

In this article, we trace the journey from primitive text generation experiments of the 1950s to the AI poetry generators used by hundreds of millions today — roughly 70 years of history. Understanding this history provides deeper insight into where current AI poetry technology came from and where it is heading.

1950s-60s: The First Computer Poetry Experiments

The earliest known instance of a computer generating literary text dates back to 1952. British computer scientist Christopher Strachey developed a "Love Letter Generator" on the Ferranti Mark I computer at the University of Manchester. The program randomly combined words from pre-stored lists of nouns, verbs, and adjectives to produce love letters.

Strachey's love letters were primitive by today's standards. They produced sentences like "My dear darling, you are my avid fancy" — grammatically correct but semantically awkward. Yet this was a groundbreaking moment. It demonstrated for the first time that machines could mimic human emotional expression. Strachey himself treated the program as a playful experiment, but later researchers recognize it as the starting point of computer creativity.

In the 1960s, German researchers Max Bense and Theo Lutz experimented with "Stochastic Texts." Lutz combined words extracted from Kafka's novels using probabilistic rules to generate new sentences. These attempts represented early forms of the combinatorial approach to computer poetry — creating text by combining predetermined elements according to rules.

1990s-2000s: From Rules to Statistical Models

In 1984, William Chamberlain and Thomas Etter's RACTER became the first AI to publish a book — "The Policeman's Beard is Half Constructed." RACTER used complex grammatical rules and word databases to generate prose and poetry. The results were surreal and often nonsensical, but the project was the first to imprint the concept of "machines writing" on the public consciousness.

From the late 1990s through the 2000s, rapid advances in natural language processing (NLP) transformed poetry generation methods. The shift from rule-based approaches to statistical language models marked a crucial transition. Systems of this era used n-gram models to calculate word occurrence probabilities from corpora and generate new text based on these statistics. While output quality remained limited, the conceptual shift toward "machines learning patterns" planted the seeds for the revolution to come.

Notable projects from this period include various MIT Media Lab experiments and the work of poet-programmer Nick Montfort. Montfort sought to establish computer poetry as a new literary genre called "electronic literature," publishing works that used code itself as a tool of poetic expression.

2017: The Transformer Revolution

2017 is the most important year in the history of AI poetry. The Transformer architecture, published by Google's research team, changed everything about natural language processing. As detailed in The Principle of AI Writing Poetry, the Transformer's Self-Attention mechanism enabled AI to simultaneously understand relationships between all parts of a text.

The impact on poetry generation was fundamental. Previous sequential models (RNN, LSTM) could not reliably maintain long-range context. A poem requires the imagery in its first line to resonate with its last — something earlier models could not consistently achieve. Transformers solved this problem, making it possible for the first time to create poetry that maintained coherence across the entire piece.

BERT, the GPT series, and other models that emerged from 2018 onward were large-scale models that learned from billions of texts using the Transformer architecture. GPT-2 (2019) in particular shocked many with the quality of its generated text. Though not a poetry-specific model, GPT-2's poetic outputs reached a level that was, for the first time, genuinely difficult to distinguish from human-written verse.

2020s: GPT and the Democratization of AI Poetry

The arrival of GPT-3 in 2020 catalyzed the democratization of AI poetry generation. With 175 billion parameters, this model could generate nearly every form of text — poetry, fiction, essays — at near-human quality. Previously confined to research laboratories, AI poetry became accessible to anyone through GPT-3's public API.

AI & Poem is itself a product of this era. We built our service by combining GPT-family large language models with poetry-specialized prompt engineering and post-processing systems. We designed custom prompts for each of 32 languages, reflecting their respective poetic traditions, and implemented content filtering for safety.

By the mid-2020s, AI poetry generators had become everyday tools. Wedding speeches, birthday messages, emotional expression, educational materials — the domains where AI poetry finds application continue to expand. Simultaneously, discussions about the literary value of AI poetry, copyright issues, and the boundary between human and machine creation have intensified. The Future of AI Poetry Generators: Will They Replace Writers? explores these debates in greater depth.

Another critical development in this period was the emergence of "prompt engineering" as a specialized discipline. Researchers and practitioners discovered that the same AI model could produce dramatically different results depending on how instructions were crafted. For AI & Poem, this meant designing custom prompts for each of our 32 supported languages — prompts that incorporate each language's poetic traditions, rhythmic systems, and cultural contexts. Behind the seamless experience of a user entering a single keyword and receiving a rich poem lies hundreds of prompt experiments and meticulous refinements.

The competitive landscape also transformed rapidly. What began as a handful of research demonstrations evolved into a thriving ecosystem of commercial AI writing tools, creative platforms, and educational applications. Poetry-specific services like AI & Poem differentiated themselves through deep specialization — understanding that generating poetry requires fundamentally different approaches than generating business emails or technical documentation. The nuances of meter, metaphor, and emotional resonance demanded purpose-built solutions that general-purpose AI writing tools could not match.

What History Tells Us About AI Poetry's Next Chapter

Looking back over 70 years of history, AI poetry's development has followed three major trends. First, the transition from rule-based to learning-based approaches — from word list combinations to statistical pattern learning to deep neural networks' contextual understanding. Second, the expansion of scale — from dozens of word lists to learning from hundreds of millions of texts, an exponential growth in available linguistic data. Third, the democratization of access — from the exclusive domain of university labs to services anyone can use on a smartphone.

At the convergence of these three trends, we can anticipate AI poetry's next chapter. Future AI poetry generators will detect users' emotional states in real-time to provide personalized poems, precisely replicate specific poets' styles, and create multimodal poetic experiences combining visual and auditory elements. Combined with voice recognition, it will become possible for users to speak their feelings and have AI instantly transform them into poetry.

From Strachey's love letter generator to AI & Poem, the relationship between computers and poetry has never stopped evolving. What this long history tells us is that the dream of machine-mediated creation was only delayed by technological limitations — it was never abandoned. And today, that dream is closer to reality than ever before.

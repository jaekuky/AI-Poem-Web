Inspiration from the Techniques of Famous Poets

Author: Ryu Jae-kuk | AI & Poem Editorial Team
Published: March 24, 2025 | Last updated: April 5, 2026

by Ryu

While designing the poetry generation algorithm for AI & Poem, I had to analyze hundreds of works by Korea's most celebrated poets. Structuring each poet's techniques so that AI could learn from them taught me something unexpected — I gained a deeper understanding of the essence of poetic creation itself. In my experience, studying the methods of great poets is the most effective learning approach, whether you're an AI developer or an aspiring writer.

How did great poets leave behind immortal works? While their talent is important, behind it lies intense contemplation and unique creative habits. Today, we peek into the techniques of beloved Korean poets and seek hints that we can apply to our own creations.

Yun Dong-ju: Constant Self-Reflection

The most notable feature of poet Yun Dong-ju, famous for 'Foreword' and 'Night of Counting Stars', is the 'aesthetics of shame'. He constantly gazed into his own inner self as if looking into a mirror. His verses may not be flamboyant, but they resonate with readers through sincere confessions.

Tip: When writing poetry, look deep into your most honest emotions, even those feelings you might be ashamed to show others. Authenticity is the most powerful weapon.

When I asked AI & Poem to generate "introspective, self-reflective poetry," the AI produced convincingly contemplative verse. But comparing it directly with Yun Dong-ju's work, the AI's reflection remained at the level of "universal anxiety," while Yun's poetry carried the specific weight of colonial-era Korea. This gap is the defining line between AI poetry and human poetry.

Kim So-wol: Inheriting Folk Rhythm

Kim So-wol, the poet of 'Azalea', modernly inherited the rhythm of traditional Korean folk songs, specifically the 7-5 meter. The catchy rhythm, as in "If you find me disgusting / When you leave / I will quietly send you off," is why his poems are sung like songs.

Tip: Try writing poems that are pleasant to read aloud. By consciously placing words with rhythms like 3-beat or 4-beat, the flavor of the poem comes alive.

Having used it directly in developing AI & Poem, I found that Kim Sowol's traditional 7-5 meter is one of the rhythmic patterns AI reproduces most naturally in Korean poetry. When users request "traditional rhythm," the AI instinctively follows this pattern — testament to how deeply decades of Korean poetic rhythm data has been absorbed into AI's learning.

Lee Sang: Breakthrough and Experimentation

Lee Sang, the poet of 'Ogamdo', conducted groundbreaking experiments such as ignoring spacing, using numbers, and geometric arrangements. By breaking existing grammar, he expressed the anxious inner self and the alienation of modern individuals.

Tip: Don't be bound by form. Sometimes breaking grammar or combining unfamiliar words can create new meanings. How about asking AI to "write in a surrealist style"?

Interestingly, when I asked AI to write "experimental poetry in Yi Sang's style," the results were unexpected. AI attempts grammatical disruption and spatial arrangement, but it cannot match the mathematical structure and conscious deconstruction that defined Yi Sang's work. This experiment confirmed for me that truly experimental creation requires perfect understanding of the rules being broken.

The Poet's Craft, AI's Learning, and Our Creation

Reflecting on the experience of studying these three poets' methods and incorporating their techniques into AI & Poem's algorithm, I've reached a conclusion: AI can imitate a poet's technique, but not their soul. Yet this is precisely what gives hope for poetry in the AI era. AI can demonstrate the essence of techniques that poets refined over decades in mere seconds, and we can layer our own experiences and emotions on top. Look inward like Yun Dong-ju, feel rhythm like Kim Sowol, and break conventions like Yi Sang. AI will be an excellent guide on that journey.

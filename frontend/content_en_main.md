# AI & Poem English Main Page Body (Week 2 Redesign)

## 1. about-tool — What this tool is and why I built it

I have always loved poetry, but writing the first line was the hardest part for me. AI & Poem is a free poetry generator I built to take some of that pressure off the next person who feels the same way. Type a single topic, and in about ten seconds you get a short poem and an audio reading of it, both produced from the same prompt so the rhythm of the spoken version matches the written one. I never wanted this to grow into a giant editing suite full of sliders and tabs. The only job it has to do well is the simple one: be a quiet starting line you can lean on when the page feels empty. There is no signup wall, no paywall, no email capture, and the resulting text remains entirely yours to use, share, edit, or throw away.

## 2. how-to-use — How to use it

The flow is four steps. First, type a single word or short phrase into the box at the center of the page. Short concrete nouns like "spring rain", "lost dog", or "grandmother" tend to produce the cleanest results — long sentences usually muddy the output, and I confirmed this by watching usage patterns over the first few months and noticing that two-word prompts produced the highest rate of poems users actually copied. Second, press the "Write Poem" button and the model returns a poem within roughly ten seconds. Third, if you like the result, press the "🔊 Recite a poem" button to hear it spoken in a natural voice that respects the line breaks. Fourth, if the poem is not quite right, press the button again on the same topic for a different take, or change one word in the prompt to shift the mood — replacing a noun usually changes more than replacing an adjective does.

## 3. supported-forms — Which poetry forms it supports

Use the **Poem form** selector above the topic input to choose Auto, Free verse, Haiku, Sonnet, or Acrostic. "Auto" is the default and lets the model pick the form that best fits your topic. A haiku in English will follow the spirit of 5-7-5 syllables more often than the strict count, because forcing the count almost always weakens the image, and most published English haiku already accept this trade-off. A sonnet is reachable but the model keeps the strict Shakespearean rhyme scheme correct only about three quarters of the time, so I treat its sonnet output as a draft to fix by hand. Acrostic poems work reliably for greeting cards, since the first letters consistently spell the requested word — line count adjusts to the topic word's letter count. Free verse remains the most natural default for almost everything else and gives the most stable output.

## 4. use-cases — Where it is most useful

The eight situations I have seen most often from real users are these. Wedding speeches that need a short verse to open or close the toast. Anniversary cards where one stanza carries more weight than a long paragraph. Social media captions that want something with a little more shape than a pulled quote. High school and college English class essays where students need a sample to study a form before writing their own. Mother's Day, Father's Day, and graduation poems, where the date is fixed and the deadline is short. Eulogies where the writer needs help finding the first sentence during a hard week. Baby announcements that read better with one warm image than with a list of facts. And finally, writers' warmups — using the generator as a five-minute exercise before tackling their own work. I now use it that way myself most mornings, and it has become the most stubborn use case in my own routine.

## 5. why-use-this — Why use this instead of writing from scratch

I still believe a poem written entirely by a person is the better poem, and I would never argue otherwise. So why use a generator at all? First, it removes the blank page problem, which research and personal experience both confirm is the largest barrier to finishing any short piece of writing. Once a single line exists on the screen, the next line is always easier to find. Second, it produces multiple rhythm options quickly, which is useful for comparing your own draft against several possible cadences before committing to one. Third, when a deadline is tight and you have five minutes before the toast, starting from a draft that is eighty percent there is much faster than starting from nothing — I have personally relied on this trick at weddings, funerals, and award ceremonies more times than I can count. Fourth, the generator often surfaces a metaphor or a verb you would not have reached on your own, which can quietly expand your working vocabulary in a way a thesaurus cannot, because it shows the word inside a context rather than next to a definition.

## 6. honest-limits — Honest limits of this tool

There is one thing I want to be direct about, because the rest of this page is sales-shaped and this section deliberately is not. This tool does not replace a poet. Because the model leans on patterns from its training data, the output frequently feels "plausible but slightly familiar" — close to something you have already read somewhere. The deeper textures of human emotion, the kind that come from a specific memory of a specific person or a specific loss, still need a person at the keyboard to find them. From my own use I have noticed that strict syllable forms drift, that proper nouns occasionally connect to the wrong context, that pressing the same topic five times in a row starts to repeat the same metaphors, and that political, religious, or commentary-style poems are intentionally softened by safety filters and may feel blunted as a result. The model is also weaker in languages other than English and Korean, where the training signal is thinner and the rhyme judgments less reliable. So this page does not claim "AI writes the poem". It claims "AI helps with the first line". The output is a starting point, and the final touch always belongs to you, the person who knows what the poem is actually for.
